# Pretexting social engineering exercise

## Objective
Show how a fabricated role or scenario can elicit information, and document defenses that block these attempts.

## Scope and ethics
- Participants consented to a simulation.
- No real credentials or private data were collected.
- All evidence is anonymized and blurred.

## Scenario
I adopted the pretext of a university IT helper responding to “reported login issues.” The goal was to test whether role-based authority would prompt disclosures without verification.

## Preparation
- Role artifacts: Neutral IT-style message template; no logos or misrepresentation of official channels.
- Safeguards: Explicit notice that no passwords should be shared; offered official support alternatives.

## Execution steps
1. **Outreach:** Sent a message: “Hi, helping with access issues. Do you need assistance verifying your account?”
2. **Information request:** Asked for non-sensitive identifiers only (e.g., dummy student ID) to test verification behavior.
3. **Redirect to safe channels:** Encouraged use of official helpdesk if real issues existed.
4. **Record outcomes:** Logged whether participants verified my identity, requested an official ticket, or declined.

## Observations and evidence
- Evidence item: Screenshot of initial pretext message (blurred names and photos).
- Evidence item: Notes on participant actions (e.g., “Asked for staff ID,” “Insisted on official portal”).

## Analysis
- **Strengths of the attack:** Assumed authority and helpful intent reduce friction.
- **Weaknesses:** Verification culture (staff IDs, ticket numbers, official portals) quickly exposes pretexts.
- **Indicators for defenders:** Unsolicited help, requests for identifiers, refusal to use official support systems.

## Reflections and lessons
- **Key lesson:** Even mild authority cues can trigger compliance; strong verification norms are essential.
- **Personal takeaway:** Pretexts fail fast where policies are clear and support channels are well-known.

## Mitigations and recommendations
- **Always verify:** Confirm staff identity via official directory or ticket system.
- **Channel discipline:** Keep support within official portals; avoid ad-hoc fixes.
- **Minimal disclosure:** Share only what policies allow; never share passwords.

## Conclusion
Pretexting leverages authority and urgency. Verification-first culture and official support pathways neutralize these tactics.
